Time resolution vs. 50-year horizon. Seshat’s main dataset is coded in coarse time slices (often ~100-year steps), so a 50-year horizon may not line up with it. Check the time-step structure of your Seshat export before you fix the label. Primary: GroupKFold (or StratifiedGroupKFold) with groups=lineage_id. Repeat with several seeds, since N is small.
Temporal (if time allows): train on t < cutoff, test on t >= cutoff. Also drop training rows whose label window crosses the cutoff, or you leak future area data into training.

Baseline: logistic regression, scaled features, L2.
Stronger model: LightGBM (or RF). Use shallow trees and strong regularization. With a few hundred polities, deep trees will overfit.

Compare FPs against the true positives (correctly flagged declines) using feature distributions and SHAP.
Look for what the FPs have that the true positives lack. This is your “what protected them” finding.
Manually review every FP case against the historical record. Some will be label artifacts (a polity renamed, a successor state, or Cliopatria area noise) rather than real survivals. Tag each FP as genuine_survival or label_artifact. This step is what separates a real audit from noise-hunting.

okk explain this in simple words Hier is the strongest separator by contribution difference. False positives have higher hierarchy values and larger positive hierarchy contributions.

Pop is similarly important. False positives have higher population values and larger positive population contributions.
Cap differs substantially, but its contribution is negative because its logistic coefficient offsets contraction risk.
Money and Info contribute somewhat more to false-positive predictions.
Gov has a negative contribution difference, meaning it contributes more toward contraction among actual contraction cases than among false positives.
The _delta features contribute very little in this small dataset.

This CSV is therefore useful for the false-positive audit: it suggests that the model often predicts contraction for historically large and highly hierarchical polities that did not meet the territory-drop label.
Important limitations:

This is a tiny sample: 5 false positives and 6 contractions.
The values use median imputation for explanation.
The model was fit on the full dataset for explanation only.
These are logistic coefficient contributions, not SHAP values.
The rankings show association with model decisions, not causal effects.
The setup
The model gives each polity-century a contraction score.
Contribution = (the feature’s weight in the model) × (that polity’s value). Positive means “pushes toward predicting contraction”. Negative means “pushes away from it”.
False positive (FP): the model predicted contraction, but the polity didn’t lose 30% of its territory.
The analysis compares 5 FPs against 6 real contractions to see what made the model wrongly confident.
What each line means
Hier (hierarchy): The biggest difference between the two groups. FPs have highly layered administrations, and the model treats that as a sign of contraction. So “very hierarchical” pushes it toward a wrong “this will shrink”.
Pop (population): The same story. FPs are large-population polities, and the model reads “big” as “likely to shrink”.
Cap (capital size): FPs do have much larger capitals. But the model’s weight for Cap is negative (big capital → less contraction), so it pushes the score down. It differs between groups but doesn’t explain the mistake. It actually works against it.
Money, Info: Slight extra push toward contraction in FPs. Minor.
Gov (governance): The sign is reversed. Gov pushes toward contraction more strongly in the real contractions than in the FPs. So it’s a feature that helps the model get true contractions right, and the FPs just don’t have it.
_delta features (changes since the last century): They barely matter in this data.
The one-sentence takeaway

The model tends to flag large, populous, highly layered polities as about to shrink, and some of those didn’t. So the question for your audit becomes: what protected those big polities? (strong succession, a successful military period, and so on, which you check by hand in CrisisDB or the literature).

Be careful with it
Only 5 FPs and 6 contractions. One polity more or less could change the ranking. Treat this as a hint about where to look, not a finding.
It may not be a pattern at all. Big, complex polities have more territory to lose, so “big → contracts” may be a real regularity that your label happens to mislabel in some cases.
Not causal. It describes what drove the model’s decision, not what drove history.
Not SHAP. These are simple coefficient × value numbers, which is fine for a logistic regression, but don’t call them SHAP in the report.
Explanation-only fit. The model was fit on the full data just to produce these numbers, so don’t quote them as performance.

For the writeup: say the contributions are “illustrative”, then pick 3-5 of the FP polities and investigate each one individually. That case-by-case work is the real audit.

whats polity

A polity is any independent political unit: a state, kingdom, empire, city-state, or similar. It’s a politically organized society that governs itself and isn’t under a higher authority.

In your data, each polity is one named entity, like the Roman Empire, the Ming Dynasty, or the Inca Empire. The term is used instead of “country” or “state” because many historical units aren’t countries in the modern sense (an empire, a chiefdom, a city-state), and the word fits all of them.

So when your analysis says “large, highly hierarchical polities,” it means big, well-organized states and empires.

A polity-century is one such polity at one point in time. Your dataset has one row per polity per century, which is why the same polity can appear many times.
